Research report · Premier League · 2015/16

The pressing forward before the spotlight.

Research questionWhat does Ayoze Pérez’s 2015/16 defensive profile reveal about an unexpected football connection with Ousmane Dembélé?

Technical summary

The model identifies Ayoze Pérez as one of the league’s clearest two-way forwards. Dembélé’s documented admiration for Pérez and later recognition as an elite pressing leader create a compelling parallel—but not evidence of direct influence.

01 / Introduction

From a Ballon d’Or season to an unexpected historical profile.

When Ousmane Dembélé won the 2025 Ballon d’Or[4] following the 2024/25 season, he was one of the clearest candidates for football’s most prestigious individual award. His goal and assist output was strong, although not exceptional when compared with recent attacking winners. Titles were also central to the result. What made his case distinctive, however, was the attention given to his defensive contribution from an advanced position. He was increasingly described in the press and on social media as a “pressing monster”, while analysis highlighted how his pressing and defensive actions helped Paris Saint-Germain complete a treble of Ligue 1, Coupe de France and Champions League titles.

This project began during exploratory work with the public StatsBomb database. I wanted to identify trends and potential areas for analysis, so I produced a plot of strikers with the highest pressing and defensive-action totals per 90 minutes. The result was surprising: Ayoze Pérez appeared first in the Defensive Intervention Index and fourth in the Pressing and Counterpressing Index. As a football fan who follows news and social-media discussion around the game, I immediately connected that result with older reports of Dembélé publicly praising Pérez.

The connection was unexpected because Dembélé could have publicly admired many more globally recognised attacking players, including Cristiano Ronaldo, Lionel Messi, Zlatan Ibrahimović, Robert Lewandowski or Harry Kane. This does not mean that Pérez directly shaped Dembélé’s style of play. Instead, it provides an interesting football narrative: a player whom Dembélé publicly admired had already produced an unusually strong defensive-forward profile in historical Premier League event data. The analysis therefore presents a reproducible data finding alongside that narrative connection, without claiming direct influence or causation.

Starting point

A forward valued without the ball

Dembélé’s recognition brought advanced defensive work into the discussion of elite attacking performance, rather than treating it as separate from attacking value.

Data discovery

Ayoze as an outlier

An exploratory striker plot identified Pérez as first for defensive interventions and fourth for pressing and counterpressing within the defined cohort.

Research boundary

Parallel, not influence

The reported admiration makes the result notable, but it cannot demonstrate that Pérez caused or taught Dembélé’s pressing behaviour.

02 / Methodology

Defensive actions need a defensive-opportunity denominator.

To create the Defensive Intervention Index and Pressing and Counterpressing Index, I did not simply divide defensive actions by total minutes played. A per-90 measure can overstate the defensive activity of a player whose team spends long periods without the ball, and understate the activity of a player at a possession-dominant team. Each event is therefore measured against the player’s estimated minutes spent out of possession while on the pitch. The resulting rates are then standardized within the 36-player Premier League striker cohort before the two indices are constructed.

Possession-adjusted event rate

For each defensive event, the rate is calculated against estimated time spent without possession rather than total minutes played.

re,30 = (Ne / Topp) × 30

re,30
Possession-adjusted rate for defensive event e, per 30 opposition-possession minutes.
Ne
Number of defensive events of type e recorded for the player.
Topp
Player’s estimated minutes spent out of possession while on the pitch.

Using total minutes would not provide equal opportunity for every striker to defend. A player at a low-possession team can spend considerably longer without the ball than a player at a possession-dominant team. The denominator therefore represents the time in which defensive actions could plausibly occur.

Estimating opposition-possession time

Opposition-possession time is reconstructed from timestamped StatsBomb events. Live-event intervals are assigned to the team in possession and counted only while the player is on the pitch. Dead-ball periods are excluded, and an interval is capped at 15 seconds to prevent sparse event logging or stoppages from being treated as continuous possession.

This remains an estimate because event data records football actions and possession labels, rather than continuous tracking data for every second of the match.

Standardisation

Each possession-adjusted event rate is standardized within the eligible Premier League striker cohort before the composite indices are calculated.

z(r) = (r − μ) / σ

z(r)
Standardized score for a possession-adjusted event rate.
r
Possession-adjusted event rate.
μ
Average rate within the eligible Premier League striker cohort.
σ
Standard deviation of that cohort’s rate.

Standardisation places different defensive actions on a common relative scale. A positive score indicates that a player performs the action more frequently than the cohort average after accounting for estimated time without the ball.

Pressing and Counterpressing Index

PCI = z(rpressure,30) + z(rcounterpress,30)

PCI
Pressing and Counterpressing Index.
rpressure,30
Possession-adjusted pressure rate.
rcounterpress,30
Possession-adjusted counterpressure rate.

This index measures a striker’s relative involvement in applying pressure during opposition possession and immediately after losing the ball. Counterpresses remain a separate component because they describe a distinct tactical behaviour: the immediate attempt to regain possession after a turnover.

Defensive Intervention Index

DII = z(rrecovery,30) + z(rinterception,30) + z(rduel,30) + 0.5z(rblock,30) − 0.5z(rdribbled-past,30)

DII
Defensive Intervention Index.
rrecovery,30
Possession-adjusted ball-recovery rate.
rinterception,30
Possession-adjusted interception rate.
rduel,30
Possession-adjusted duel rate.
rblock,30
Possession-adjusted block rate.
rdribbled-past,30
Possession-adjusted rate of being dribbled past.

This index captures defensive outcomes beyond pressing. Recoveries, interceptions and duels receive full weight; blocks receive half-weight because they are less common, while being dribbled past receives a half-weight penalty as a negative defensive outcome.

Interpretation boundary. Both indices are within-cohort, possession-adjusted profiles rather than universal player ratings. The adjustment improves opportunity fairness, but it cannot fully equalize pressing scheme, field position or opponent buildup style.

His pressure volume is supported by immediate reactions after possession was lost, not only settled defensive actions.

03 / Analysis

The connection is meaningful as a parallel—not as causal proof.

The independent pieces align: your historical model identifies Pérez as an unusually active defensive striker; official tactical analysis now recognizes Dembélé for initiating pressure; and secondary reporting on Dembélé’s 2014–15 social posts[3] documents repeated public praise for Pérez. Together they create a distinctive football story.

What the data supports

Ayoze was a two-way outlier in this defined striker cohort: fourth for pressing and first for interventions. The result is reproducible from the project’s event-data methodology.

What industry evidence supports

Dembélé later became publicly recognized for leading an elite press, with UEFA and PSG describing both his tactical responsibility and personal commitment to the action.

What the admiration adds

It supplies a human connection between two players whose reputations include intensive defensive work from advanced positions.

What it cannot establish

There is no evidence here that Pérez taught, caused or directly shaped Dembélé’s pressing. Their teams, eras, roles and data definitions are different.

Source caution. The Ayoze admiration story is supported by multiple reports describing resurfaced social posts, but the original posts are not preserved here as a primary archive. It also is not a tactical interview in which Dembélé names Pérez as the source of his pressing behaviour. “Publicly praised” or “publicly admired” is therefore more defensible than “tactical idol” or “influence.”

04 / Conclusion

An unexpected stylistic echo.

A player admired by Dembélé was already displaying the defensive-forward profile that would later become central to Dembélé’s own reputation.

The value of the finding is not a claim of imitation. It is the convergence of a reproducible historical result, an unusual personal connection and modern tactical recognition. That combination turns Ayoze’s position on the chart into a stronger piece of football storytelling.

How to strengthen the report

  • Apply the same event definitions to a full Dembélé season.
  • Compare both players using percentile ranks rather than raw cross-provider counts.
  • Video-code pressing triggers, direction and recoveries caused.
  • Preserve the original social posts or a stronger primary source for the admiration claim.

Final interpretation. Report this as a narrative parallel supported by separate evidence streams—not as proof of a mentor, lineage or causal relationship.

Research report · Top five leagues · 2017/18

How different were Europe’s possession styles?

Research questionTo what extent did attacking possession styles differ across England, Spain, Italy, France and Germany during the complete 2017/18 domestic seasons?

Technical summary

The leagues shared a remarkably similar possession-style structure. Differences exist at the margins, but the evidence supports convergence more strongly than five distinct national identities.

623,321Possessions reconstructed
205,879Eligible possessions clustered

01 Context02 Methodology03 Analysis04 Conclusion
01 / Context

A shared season makes comparison possible.

The public Wyscout event dataset contains one complete and directly comparable season for each of Europe’s top five leagues. Rather than claiming how leagues changed over time, this study asks whether their attacking possessions developed in meaningfully different ways within the same 2017/18 window.

Comparison

Five domestic leagues

England, Spain, Italy, France and Germany are evaluated through the same possession definitions and feature pipeline.

Unit of analysis

One attacking possession

Styles are learned from eligible open-play sequences, rather than league totals, final scores or subjective tactical labels.

Interpretation

Descriptive, not causal

The results describe pattern prevalence. They do not establish that league membership causes a team to play in a particular way.

02 / Methodology

Cluster how attacks develop—not whether they score.

The model deliberately excludes shots, goals, teams and leagues from its clustering inputs. Outcomes are added back only after the styles are discovered, reducing the risk that the algorithm simply separates successful possessions from unsuccessful ones.

01

Reconstruct possession

Controlled actions signal ownership. Mirrored opponent duel records do not automatically switch possession; shots, stoppages, restarts and period boundaries close sequences.

02

Define the cohort

Keep open-play possessions containing at least three recorded actions and one pass. Set-piece starts are excluded from the default run.

03

Prepare tactical features

Continuous variables are median-filled, transformed when skewed, winsorized at the 1st and 99th percentiles, and standardized.

04

Reduce dimensionality

PCA is fitted on a league-balanced sample and retains 12 components, preventing larger leagues from dominating the learned space.

05

Select the model

Repeated K-means++ solutions from K=3 to K=8 are compared using silhouette, Davies–Bouldin, inertia and minimum cluster share.

06

Profile after clustering

The selected K=6 model is applied to all eligible possessions; league prevalence and shot/goal rates are calculated afterward.

Validation note. K=6 achieved the highest sampled silhouette score (0.149) while keeping the smallest cluster above the 3% threshold (4.7%). The modest silhouette value indicates overlapping styles rather than perfectly separated tactical categories.

03 / Analysis

League identity changes the mix less than expected.

Read each heatmap row as the composition of one league’s eligible possessions. The same three styles dominate every competition, and the range between leagues is narrow. The visual therefore supports a conclusion of subtle variation inside a common tactical structure.

Patient circulation dominates everywhere.

It accounts for 32.5% to 35.2% of eligible possessions across all five leagues—a spread of only 2.7 percentage points.

Transitions and recycling form a shared second tier.

Fast transition ranges from 22.4% to 23.9%, while recycling possession ranges from 22.6% to 24.6%.

Direct progression shows the clearest league contrast.

Germany (7.6%) and England (7.4%) sit above Italy (5.2%), but even this largest visible contrast remains modest.

Rare does not mean ineffective.

Line-breaking combinations represent only 4.7% overall, yet have the highest post-clustering shot rate (16.7%) and goal rate (3.43%).

04 / Conclusion

Different at the margins; similar at the core.

To a limited extent, the top five leagues displayed different possession styles in 2017/18.

The ordering and scale of the dominant clusters remain strikingly consistent. National competition alone appears to explain less stylistic variation than familiar league stereotypes might suggest. This conclusion applies to aggregate league distributions—not every club or match within them.

What should come next

  • Compare clubs within leagues to test whether team identity outweighs league identity.
  • Bootstrap league shares to quantify uncertainty around percentage-point differences.
  • Run sensitivity checks on possession boundaries, thresholds and set-piece inclusion.
  • Review and personalize the rule-based tactical labels before publication.

Limitations. The dataset covers one season per league; the styles overlap; and the labels are interpretation aids rather than ground truth. The analysis is descriptive and should not be read as a causal ranking of leagues.

Research report · Premier League & La Liga · 2015/16

Do high-scoring strikers defend less?

Research questionAfter adjusting for time spent without the ball, is striker defensive involvement associated with goal output and final team position?

Technical summary

The apparent relationship weakens after defensive opportunity is equalized. Elite scorers often record fewer actions per 90 partly because stronger teams spend less time without the ball—not simply because their strikers contribute less when defending.

01 / Context

Raw defensive volume confuses behaviour with opportunity.

Several of the season’s leading scorers sit low on the original defensive charts. That could reflect individual role, but it could also reflect team strength: a striker cannot make defensive actions while his own team controls the ball. The analysis therefore asks whether the pattern remains after giving every player the same estimated amount of time without possession.

Population

Two complete leagues

Thirty-six Premier League and thirty-three La Liga central forwards with at least 1,200 minutes are evaluated under the same definitions.

Outcome

Goals per 90

Goals are taken directly from StatsBomb shot outcomes; final league positions are reconstructed from all 380 results in each competition.

Interpretation

Association, not effort

The rate describes recorded defensive involvement under estimated exposure. It does not measure motivation or establish that scoring changes defensive behaviour.

02 / Methodology

Choose a denominator that represents time available to defend.

The primary metric is defensive actions per 30 estimated opposition-possession minutes. It combines pressures, duels, ball recoveries, interceptions and blocks, then divides by the reconstructed time the player’s team spent without the ball while he was on the pitch.

01

Why not per 90?

Per 90 corrects for playing time but not opportunity. A striker in a possession-dominant team naturally has fewer defensive minutes available.

02

Why not per 100 possessions?

Possession counts treat a five-second sequence and a two-minute sequence as equal, although the longer possession creates far more defensive exposure.

03

Why opposition-possession time?

Duration preserves the unequal length of possessions. Scaling to 30 minutes makes players comparable over the same estimated time without the ball.

04

Reconstruct the clock

Timestamped live-event intervals are assigned to the recorded possession team and intersected with each player’s on-pitch interval. Dead-ball gaps are excluded.

05

Bound uncertainty

Intervals are capped at 15 seconds so stoppages or sparse logging do not become possession time. Ten- and twenty-second caps provide sensitivity checks.

06

Test the relationships

League-specific Spearman correlations compare the adjusted activity, pressing and intervention measures with goals per 90 and final team position.

Metric-design principle. The denominator should match the opportunity required for the numerator. Opposition-possession time is estimated because event data records actions and possession labels—not a continuous tracking-data stopwatch.

03 / Analysis

Opportunity explains part of the original pattern.

The negative goals relationship remains in both leagues, but becomes weaker after the adjustment. The evidence therefore supports a restrained conclusion: team possession contributed to the low per-90 defensive totals of several leading scorers, while tactical role and individual behaviour still create meaningful exceptions.

Premier League: −0.11

The adjusted activity–goals correlation is weaker than the original per-90 value of −0.17. The team-position relationship falls from +0.20 to +0.07.

La Liga: −0.16

The adjusted activity–goals correlation is weaker than the original −0.23. The team-position relationship falls from +0.33 to +0.26.

Roles still matter.

Griezmann remains a strong defensive contributor alongside substantial goal output, while several elite scorers remain low after adjustment.

The timing choice is stable.

Player rankings correlate at 0.998 or higher across 10-, 15- and 20-second interval caps, so the ordering is not driven by one arbitrary threshold.

04 / Conclusion

Less opportunity—not simply less defending.

Leading scorers tended to record less defensive activity, but the association is weak and becomes smaller after accounting for time without the ball.

The original per-90 view overstated part of the relationship because it compared players with unequal defensive exposure. The adjusted metric produces a fairer role comparison while preserving genuine tactical outliers.

What should come next

  • Add opponent buildup passes and field position.
  • Model goals and team strength together.
  • Cluster uncertainty by club.
  • Compare adjusted rankings with video-coded pressing roles.

Limitations. Opposition-possession minutes are event-derived estimates; the adjustment does not equalize field position, pressing scheme or opponent buildup style. Results are descriptive and league-specific.

The approach

Data with a point of view.

Marco FutStats turns event data into clear visual stories. The aim is not to flatten football into a single number, but to make tactical tendencies, player roles and outlier performances easier to see—and more interesting to debate.

How each project uses the stack

The same core workflow is adapted to each football question rather than applying one generic technique to every dataset.

Striker defensive profiles

StatsBomb actions are aggregated by player, while event timestamps and possession teams reconstruct time spent without the ball. Defensive rates are scaled to 30 estimated opposition-possession minutes, tested against alternative timing caps and standardized within each league.

Midfielder passing profiles

Wyscout lineups, substitutions and event archives are reconstructed into player minutes. Smart-pass and key-pass rates are standardized within curated central-midfield cohorts before static and interactive rendering.
